    Why monitor arms fail — and how a two-minute check prevents it

    A monitor arm looks simple: a clamp, a jointed arm, and a plate that screws to the back of your screen. Yet returns and drooping monitors are common, and almost always for one of three boring reasons: the monitor is heavier than the arm is rated for, the screen is larger than the arm is rated for, or the monitor has no VESA mounting holes at all. None of these are visible from a product photo, and all of them are checkable in two minutes from a spec sheet. This calculator runs all three checks at once and tells you which one fails, if any, so you can fix the right problem instead of guessing.

    The stakes are higher than a wobbly screen. A gas-strut arm that is overloaded will not hold its height: you set the monitor at eye level, let go, and it sinks. Over weeks that sag strains the joints and can end with the monitor dropping onto the desk. A mechanical spring arm may simply refuse to stay up. Either way the monitor, and anything beneath it, is at risk. Weight is also the least forgiving spec: manufacturers rate arms for a range, and a monitor at the very top of that range leaves no margin for cables, accessories, or the small extra weight of an adapter plate. Aim for at least 20 percent headroom where possible.

    The three checks, explained

    Weight check: monitor weight without its stand must be less than or equal to the arm's maximum weight rating. Use the without-stand figure, because the arm replaces the stand; the with-stand figure overstates the load by a kilogram or more and may push you to a heavier, pricier arm than you need. Size check: screen size in inches must be within the arm's rated maximum. This matters less for weight than for geometry: an oversized screen can collide with the desk, the wall, or a second monitor when tilted, and its centre of gravity sits further from the mount, increasing leverage on the joints. VESA check: the monitor must have a standard VESA hole pattern, or you must buy an adapter. The two common patterns are 75×75 millimetres and 100×100 millimetres, measured between hole centres. Larger monitors may use 200×100 or 200×200 millimetres, which many arms support with an included plate — check before ordering.

    Gas-strut arms and mechanical arms behave differently at the edge of their range. A gas strut is happiest near the middle of its rated range: too light a monitor and the arm floats upward; too heavy and it sinks. If your monitor is near either end of the range, check reviews for that specific combination, or choose an arm whose range centres on your monitor's weight. Mechanical arms with a tension screw are more forgiving of light monitors but need periodic re-tensioning as springs settle. Either type should move smoothly with one hand once adjusted; if you need two hands and force, something is wrong with the fit or the tension.

    A worked example

    Take a 27 inch monitor weighing 6 kilograms without its stand, and an arm rated to 8 kilograms and 32 inches with a 100×100 millimetre VESA plate. Weight check: 6 ≤ 8, pass, with 2 kilograms of headroom (25 percent). Size check: 27 ≤ 32, pass. VESA check: the monitor has 100×100 holes, pass. Verdict: fits, comfortably. Now change one fact: the same monitor on an arm rated to 5 kilograms. Weight fails by a kilogram, and no amount of size or VESA compatibility fixes it — the arm will droop. Change a different fact instead: a monitor with no VESA holes. Weight and size pass, but the mount has nothing to screw into, so you need an adapter plate that clamps or adheres to the monitor, and that adapter's own rating becomes part of the check.

    Consider a heavier case: a 49 inch super ultrawide weighing 14 kilograms. Many popular arms top out at 9 or 10 kilograms and will not hold it at all, regardless of size rating. The right arm for that screen is typically a heavy-duty single arm rated to 15–20 kilograms, or a pole mount with a reinforced bracket. The calculator's headroom figure is the quickest way to see this: if headroom is negative, stop. If it is small, under 1 kilogram, treat the fit as marginal and look for a stronger arm or a lighter monitor configuration.

    Desk and mounting checks people forget

    • Clamp range: measure desk thickness and compare with the clamp's stated range. A clamp that is too small will not close; one that is too large may not grip.
    • Desk material: avoid clamping to glass tops, hollow-core doors used as desks, or very thin particle board without a reinforcement plate underneath.
    • Grommet mounts: if your desk has a cable hole, a grommet mount can be stronger and neater than a clamp, but check the hole diameter.
    • Wall mounts: must hit a stud or solid masonry, not plasterboard alone. Use a stud finder and proper fixings.
    • Clearance: swing the arm through its full range mentally: will the monitor hit the wall, a shelf, or a second screen at full tilt?

    Ergonomic setup once it fits

    • Set the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away.
    • Tilt the screen back very slightly, 10–20 degrees, so you look slightly downward at the centre.
    • Centre the monitor on your keyboard, not off to one side, to avoid neck rotation.
    • For dual monitors, angle them in a shallow V with the primary screen directly ahead.
    • Route cables with slack: tight cables pull on the arm and restrict movement.

    Common mistakes

    The most common mistake is using the with-stand weight, which overstates the load and leads to overspending — or, worse, using a guessed weight that understates it. The second is assuming every monitor has VESA holes; always check the back or the spec sheet, especially for curved, Apple, and budget models. The third is ignoring the size rating for ultrawides, whose width and leverage stress arms more than their weight alone suggests. The fourth is clamping to an unsuitable desk and blaming the arm when the desk flexes. A monitor arm is a system: monitor, arm, mount, and desk must all fit together, and this calculator checks the first three so you can focus on the fourth.

    When to choose a different mount

    If the calculator says no on weight, buy a stronger arm rather than hoping the rating is conservative — it rarely is by enough. If it says no on VESA, buy the correct adapter first and re-check with the adapter's weight included. If it says no on size but weight passes, check whether the size limit is about collision rather than strength: sometimes a pole mount or a wall mount with more clearance solves it. And if your desk cannot take a clamp, consider a freestanding monitor stand or a wall mount into a stud. The right mount is the one that passes every check, not just most of them.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is VESA?

    VESA is the standard square hole pattern on the back of a monitor for mounting. Most monitors use 75×75 mm or 100×100 mm; larger screens may use 200×100 mm or 200×200 mm.

    How do I find my monitor's weight?

    Check the manufacturer's spec sheet for weight without the stand, since the arm replaces the stand. Weighing it on a bathroom scale also works, stand removed.

    What happens if the arm is under-rated?

    A gas-strut arm may droop or fail to hold height, and a mechanical arm may sag. In the worst case the mount can fail and drop the monitor.

    Do I need to check desk thickness?

    Yes for clamp mounts: check the clamp's range against your desk thickness, and avoid clamping to thin glass or hollow-core tops without reinforcement.

    Can one arm hold two monitors?

    Dual arms exist, but each side has its own weight and size limit. Check each monitor against its own side, not the combined total.

    What if my monitor has no VESA holes?

    Some monitors, especially certain Apple and curved models, need a brand-specific adapter plate. Buy the adapter before the arm, and verify its own weight rating.
